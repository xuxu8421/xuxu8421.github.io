THE USER PROBLEM

A lower score does not explain what to fix.

For algorithm and evaluation teams, a benchmark result is a starting point. They still need to understand which failures recur, which capabilities those failures point to, and what kinds of training data may help.

I led product design for the analysis module in DataSeek, organizing a path from individual errors to findings a researcher could inspect and use. I paired requirements and workflow decisions with a Python/LangGraph prototype so I could test the analysis as well as describe it.

PRODUCT DECISIONS

Three decisions behind a more useful report.

1. Let the evidence determine the analysis path.

Datasets do not all supply the same evidence. I designed four analysis modes around whether reference answers and scoring rubrics were present, then encoded the corresponding reasoning and diagnosis constraints in structured prompts.

The product decision was to make those conditions explicit instead of treating every dataset as if it had the same reference information.

2. Separate error diagnosis from capability aggregation.

Question-level findings can be too vague, too fragmented, or repetitive to support a decision. I defined capability names around the task scenario and action, with constraints against unsupported generalization.

I led iterations on bucketing, within-bucket aggregation, and deduplication. The aim was to retain specific failure evidence while giving users a stable level at which to compare capability gaps.

3. Distinguish the main failure cause from skills merely involved.

A task can involve several capabilities while failing mainly because of one. Combining those signals into a single count can mislead prioritization. I designed separate primary-cause and involved-question counts and carried that distinction through prompt and prototype work to engineering acceptance.

PRIMARY-CAUSE QUESTIONSWhy did the model fail?

Questions attributed to the capability as a main failure cause.

INVOLVED QUESTIONSWhat did the task require?

Questions involving the capability, even when another issue drove the failure.

The distinction clarifies the meaning of the counts; it does not make automated attribution a ground truth.

I owned product requirements, analysis logic, prompt design, prototype implementation, and report review. For capability aggregation, my work covered problem discovery, solution design, prompt and prototype development, and engineering acceptance.

I worked with engineers on production delivery, providing templates for operator decomposition and following requirements and fixes through review. Recurring failures became concrete issues and acceptance criteria rather than open-ended requests to “improve the model.”

I also distinguished immediate display fallbacks from longer-term prompt and analysis changes: one made a report usable in the near term; the other addressed the behavior producing the failure.

WORK DELIVERED

Product design backed by repeated evaluation.

Across the internship, I led module design and iterative releases, built and revised the LangGraph prototype, authored 30+ structured prompts, and led cumulative manual reviews of 700+ reports. Nearly 100 prioritized issues connected report failures to product requirements, prompt changes, and engineering follow-up.

What I would measure next

The next product question is whether a finding changes the researcher’s decision: can they trace it to concrete errors, agree with the diagnosis, and turn it into a useful data task? I would pair those signals with reviewer agreement and time-to-action.
